# Ages of Earth

A real-time strategy game in the browser, in the spirit of Empire Earth and Age of Empires: lead your people from the Stone Age through the Bronze Age, the Middle Ages and the Gunpowder Age to the Industrial Age, gather food, wood, stone and gold, fish the seas, build a town, raise an army and a navy, take to the air and the atom, and conquer your rivals, alone against the computer or online with friends. It is built with TypeScript and Three.js, the same stack and low-poly look as Tideborn, and deploys the same way: a Cloudflare Worker serving static files, ready for a subdomain of your own domain.

What players love most about the classics, and where to find it here, is written up in [docs/design-notes.md](docs/design-notes.md).

## What's in it

- **Reading the rival's road.** A rival's age change is a notice you can click, and a **Rivals** button keeps a card for each: what its age is good at and shuts, the road it has walked, a countdown over its weak window after a step back (its soldiers fall back to their older form), and any crossing it is half-way to. A rival building you have seen that keeps an older age's work (a Forge in a Stone Age town) is ringed on the minimap and named: "they have walked the Iron Age".
- **The road planner.** A **Roads** button in the top bar (remembered per browser) lists every road you know from where you stand with its price, time and what leaving keeps, sleeps, wakes and loses, and the step back (Z). Pin a goal age (from the panel, or the 📍 on the web map) and it plans the shortest journey by time, step-backs and their cooldown included, with what each leg still lacks; half-walked crossings show as goals.
- **Your road at the end.** The end screen draws every player's journey through the web of ages: the climbs, the steps back (dashed), time in each age, whether a rival struck the weak window after each step back, keepsakes carried and crossings walked, and the roads you knew of that nobody took. **Play this seed again** replays the same map, peoples and rivals.
- **Rivals with a leaning.** In a skirmish, many clever computer rivals favour one route (army, economy, horse, faith, wild; navy and water where there is water) and keep to it; one with an open mind that builds in stone in the Megaliths sets out on the Deep Road and walks it down to the Living Earth. Once its road gives the leaning away, its card in **Rivals** names it; the end screen always does. A clever computer also strikes when you step back: while your weak window is open, a smaller wave than usual comes for your Town Center.
- **A first minute of your own.** A new match opens with a card: your people's loop and first move, the land you woke in, and the Way your people lean to; a minute on, a tip about that people's own loop (`src/ui/opening.ts`).
- **Raider camps.** Out between the towns (one on a small map, two or three on bigger ones) a band of three raiders keeps watch over a fire: spearmen in hide kilts with red shoulder paint, no one's people. Anyone who comes within a few tiles sets them all on you, and they chase no further than their fire. Citizens alone lose; a few soldiers win, and whoever fells the **last** raider takes the camp's plunder (120 gold, 80 food, 60 wood). So the early game has a real question: strike the camp now for a head start (and risk your soldiers), or leave it and boom. A Normal or harder computer with four idle soldiers marches on a camp within reach (unless a rival's army stands by it). The camp's own tip appears when you first see one; a banner and a coin chime mark the plunder. `RAIDER` in `data.ts`; no save change.
- **Tidings at the fire.** Twice in the Stone Age (about 2½ and 5 minutes in, the seed sets the moment) a parchment card comes to your camp with a choice and a price: a lone traveller asks to stay (50 food: a citizen more, if a house has room), a hunter offers 70 meat for 40 wood, a hungry stranger knows where a raider camp lies (25 food: it lies open on your map), a storm gathers (40 wood to shore up the huts, or chance it: on half the seeds every house loses 40% of its health), a fever comes (40 food to tend the sick, or let it run: on half the seeds a citizen dies). The two cards differ, and differ by seed. Leave a card and after 30 seconds it passes with the do-nothing answer. A drum and horn mark its coming. The computer answers too, paying when the price leaves it enough. Not in campaign chapters, nor once a people has left the Stone Age. `TIDINGS` in `data.ts`; no save change.
- **Mine Shafts.** From the Bronze Age a **Mine Shaft** (economy menu, **H**, 150 wood) digs stone or gold from the ground for good, once the open deposits are gone. One miner works it (right-click with a citizen) and carries the load to a drop site. The ground sets the pace: old workings (a deposit worked out nearby) 75% of an open deposit, mountain rock beside it 50%, plain ground 30%. Select the shaft to choose the seam; salt, saltpetre and blood-iron are dug only over their own old workings. A household people mines no gold and a nomad people quarries no stone. The computer sinks shafts when nothing lies near its drops and staffs them. A tip appears when home runs dry. `SHAFT` and `BUILDINGS.shaft` in `data.ts`; no save change.
- **God mode (testing).** In a solo match, type `Anissa1030` (there is no button to enter): it is a sandbox: nothing changes on entry, then the treasury stays full, builds, research and ages finish instantly, and an age picker in the top bar jumps to any age, forward or back. A **God mode: exit** button leaves it. Never available online or in replays.
- **Great works.** Once in each epoch from the Bronze Age, select a Town Center and press **V** to call your people's great work: free, for 90 seconds. Egyptians: the Shaduf Sweeps (farms +60%). Greeks: the Phalanx (foot +2 armor, +2 attack). Romans: the Roman Road (building 75% faster, citizens +25% speed). Mongols: the Yam Relay (cavalry +40% speed and +3 sight, citizens +15%). Chinese: the Grand Canal (trade pays +50%, traders +30% faster). Britons: the Longbow Volley (ranged +3 attack, ranges train twice as fast). Spend it on the boom or hold it for the battle; Hard and Expert computers time theirs (a farming or trading people while busy at it, a fighting one when a wave marches or a raid arrives). Its own horn-and-bells sound, and while it runs motes in the people's colors rise from its Town Centers, so you can see a rival's work is on; no save change.
- **Homelands (#473).** Each seed rolls what lies round every town, the same for every player: the **Glade** (berries and a deer herd, the old start), the **Shore** (a fishing mere by home and a thin berry bush: a Dock and canoes are the food), the **Herd Plains** (two deer herds and a mammoth close, few berries: a meat economy) or the **Deep Wood** (woods crowding in and a wide berry thicket, no herd near). Which ones come up depends on the map type (more Herd Plains on Plains, more Deep Wood in the Black Forest, more Shore on the Great Lakes and the Inland Sea). Pick one on the title screen or leave it to the seed; the opening banner, the clock's tooltip and a first tip name it, and the computer reads it (a Dock first on the Shore). Old saves, replays and campaign chapters keep the Glade.
- **Herds breed (#491).** Hunting no longer runs the land dry: each spring every wild herd of two or more deer drops a calf for each pair, up to six a herd. Spare a pair and the herd comes back; a lone deer breeds nothing.
- **The turning year (#479).** Each homeland's year turns its staple, so the opening that suits it stops working for part of every year. On the **Herd Plains** the home herds stay home through spring and summer, trek out toward the middle of the map each autumn (follow them with a Hunting Camp, or eat from the store and the fields) and come home each spring, every deer still alive bringing a calf, up to six a herd: hunt a herd out and it never comes back, and a rival can hunt yours on its autumn grass. On the **Shore** the shoals run thick in autumn (boats fish 30% faster) and the mere ices over in winter (a third of the pace). In the **Deep Wood**, as the berries fail each winter, five deer come in from the cold to yard in the woods by every town and go back out in spring, the same ones each year while they live. The **Glade** keeps the old year. The season's banner, the clock's tooltip and a tip a season ahead say what's coming; the clever computer spares two of each home herd, follows its herds out, plans fields before the ice and sends foragers to hunt the yard (woodcutters too, when it has no fields to spare), and its hunters shy from a rival's soldiers.
- **The mere spawns (#482).** On the **Shore**, each spring every shoal still in the water grows by as much as is left in it (up to full), and a full one seeds a new half-full shoal beside it; a shoal fished to nothing never grows back (a full neighbour may seed its place), and a mere stripped bare stays bare. So the autumn run asks whether to strip the mere for food now or move the boats on while each shoal keeps a seed. A toast says what the spring brought; the clever computer moves its boats off a shoal once it is down to 60 fish, unless it is hungry.
- **Snares in the wood (#485).** A **Snare** (economy menu, J, 30 wood) goes only among standing trees, 5 tiles from any other. It fills with small game by itself, faster the more trees stand round it and half again faster in winter, up to 24 food, and a hare lies in the noose once there is a load in it. Right-click one with a citizen to walk the trap line: empty each snare, go on to the next, carry the catch home. Fell the trees round a snare and it starves, so the woods you keep are food and the woods you cut are wood. A Warband soldier who strikes a rival's snare carries off its catch. The computer lays a line in the Deep Wood.
- **Ways.** In the Stone Age, select a Town Center and pick a **Way**, a custom your people live by for the whole match: free, once. Each Way changes what you do and shuts a door (#463 s1). The **Hunters' Way** camps where the kill falls: a Hunting Camp within 6 tiles of game or a fresh carcass costs nothing and goes up three times as fast (hunting +25%, beasts bite 30% softer, berries −10%), but in the Stone Age it **sows nothing where the herd grazes**: no Farm while deer, boars or mammoths live within 24 tiles of its Town Center, so hunt the home herd out to open the fields, or spare it and range for meat; on the Herd Plains a spared herd treks away each autumn and its ground lies open to the plough till spring (the town's own herd only: wild game and a rival's herd still shut the fields), so a Hunter there can hunt the calves in spring and sow in autumn (#529). The **Hearth Way** **calls the kin** at a Town Center (120 food: 2 citizens together after 16 s, **3 in winter**, so food is laid by for the lean season; berries +20%, farms +10%, wood +10%), but keeps **no Hunting Camps** (hunting −20%, beasts bite 20% harder). The **Warband Way** **feeds on the raid**: every blow its soldiers land on a rival Town Center, Storehouse, Hunting Camp, Dock or Farm carries off food from the rival's store, and a slain rival citizen gives up its load (soldiers train 15% faster and strike +1), but **its bell never rings** and its citizens never shelter (berries and hunting −15%). Look at the deer and berries round your town and at how near your rival is. The computer leans to its people's own Way (Mongols and Britons hunt, Egyptians and Chinese keep the hearth, Romans and Greeks raid) and weighs the land; it pitches free kill camps (and, as a Hunter, keeps off the Steppe, whose flocks start from a store it lacks), calls the kin and sends an early raiding band. Leave the Stone Age without choosing and the moment is gone. Rivals are told which Way you took; a clever computer picks by the deer, berries and rival near its home; the choice shows on your people's chip at the top. **Vows (#338):** once, in the Bronze Age, a Town Center offers to **deepen** your Way (200 food + 100 wood: its gifts and its price both grow) or **turn** to another (100 food: the old custom is dropped). Pass it by and the Way stays; a clever computer deepens when it can pay with a stock to spare. No save change. **Way marks:** a people's Way shows at its Town Center (a pelt frame and stag skull, a stone-ringed fire, or a rack of spears and a red shield), so scouting a town tells you how it plays.  Each Way has its own sound when chosen.

- **The Atomic Age and the Missile Silo** (#391, #605). A sixth epoch past the Industrial (1800 food, 1500 gold, 700 stone, 95 seconds). Its building is the **Missile Silo** (M in the military menu; 300 wood, 400 stone, 700 gold): select it, press **M** (Launch) and click anywhere on the map. The warhead lands 45 seconds later and blasts every rival building and soldier within 8 tiles: 12,000 damage at the centre, falling off as 12,000 ÷ (1 + (d÷2)²) to nothing at the edge (a building is measured to its nearest edge; sheltered units are spared), so a direct hit finally breaks a Town Center; your own people are spared. Fission's warhead is 20,000 over 10 tiles, Fusion's 40,000 over 12 (`WARHEADS`). The blast is seen by **every** player through the fog: the flash, the boom, a minimap ping, and the fog stays lifted over its radius for a minute for all, so everyone can watch what burned and who moves in. Everyone sees the clock (a ☢ chip in the top bar, a toast), and a silo destroyed while it counts scrubs the launch. **The three-zone warning** (#762): a rival never sees the target, but **three red rings** on the ground and the minimap, the real zone and two decoys drawn from the match's own dice where the attacker might have aimed (weighted by what a strike would take of the towns and armies the attacker had scouted), and every ring set off by up to 2 tiles from its point, the real one too, so even a ring's centre is a guess. Each rival team gets its own set. With exactly 3 seconds left the decoys vanish and the real ring flashes where it was drawn, with one alarm; the blast still lands on the true point. So the defender chooses: spread out, guess the ring and save the rest, or race for the silo. The attacker sees only its own aim, in amber; the computer defender runs from the three rings it is shown, never the hidden point. The 5 tiles round the point then stay **fallout** for 90 seconds: every land unit in it, anyone's, loses 4 health a second (buildings are spared; a green haze marks it), so a flattened town can't be marched into at once, and the computer pulls its people out of it. A launch costs 600 gold and 200 stone in fuel. **The missile pool** (#753): warheads come from one store a people keeps, not from any silo: one when the first silo stands, one more every 240 seconds while the store and the warheads in the air number under 3, spent the moment a launch is ordered and fired from any standing silo. A fallen silo takes no stored warhead with it, only the one it is counting down. A people raises at most 2 silos (3 with Launch Control), and every player sees a finished one through the fog, with no alert; the store itself stays hidden. **The salvo** (#765): **Launch Control** (researched at a silo: 600 food, 900 gold, 400 stone, 60 s) allows a third silo, and three standing silos may fire a **salvo** (B, then click three points at least 8 tiles apart): three Atomic warheads from the store at once, one from each silo, on the people's one clock, for three times the Atomic fuel (half as much again on each for a plain Atomic people; past the fork the plain price). A rival sees three rings drawn by the same rule as a single strike's, one launch, one chip, one toast, and learns that all three are real only in the last 3 seconds, when all three stay and flash. Each silo destroyed before then scrubs its own warhead and its ring; the others fly on and each lands on its true point. So three silos on the map are a threat a defender must read, and the attacker weighs one great strike against three that break a spread-out people. A clever computer learns Launch Control and raises three silos once three rival towns are each worth a warhead, holds its single strikes for the salvo while it pays well more than one strike, and fires it. `SALVO`, `AI_SILO` in `data.ts`. **The flight** (#772): every launch is a missile every player watches, through the fog: a plume of fire and smoke at the silo, then a pale rocket climbing on a high arc with a trail of smoke. For most of its flight it heads for the middle of the warning rings, so it tells the defender a district, not a point; some 8 to 12 seconds before the reveal (more on a long flight) it bends toward where it falls and dives. A defender who reads the turn can clear the right ring early, or trust it and stay; nothing ahead of the missile is drawn. A salvo climbs as one missile from its first silo and splits into three as it bends. A clever computer reads the bend too, a few seconds in, and stops clearing the rings the course has ruled out. `FLIGHT` in `data.ts`. So the late game gains a question the army can't answer by size alone: guard the silo, scatter before the clock runs out, or raid theirs. A clever computer raises one silo, launches at the spot that catches the most of a rival's town and soldiers, and runs its people out of a rival warhead's ring. Aeronauts and Lightning stay side roads off the Industrial. `SILO`, `AI_SILO` in `data.ts`; saves from before the sixth epoch still load. **The fork past it** (#605 s3, seed #533): a Missile Silo is the clue to two turns off the Atomic Age that shut each other for good. **Age of Fission** (1000 food, 1200 gold, 600 stone, 60 s): the clock is 25 seconds and a warhead is made every 100, but each launch poisons 3 tiles round your own silo for a minute, as fallout does, so silos go far from home. **Age of Fusion** (2400 food, 3000 gold, 1500 stone, 150 s, so the town is exposed while it climbs): up to three warheads are aloft at once and a new one is made the moment one lands, a launch costs 450 gold and 120 stone, and nothing it strikes is poisoned so an army can follow the blast in, but the clock is 70 seconds. Past the fork the warhead is chosen at launch (M for the branch's own, N for Atomic): a Fission people weighs a cheap Atomic (600 gold) against its heavy Fission (800 gold, 250 stone), a Fusion people its clean giant against an Atomic that poisons the ground; the clock is the people's whatever it sends (25, 45 or 70 s), and the warning ring is the widest it could send, so neither gives the warhead away. Each has a cheap road back to the Atomic Age. A computer with a silo takes a branch on a coin of its own. `FISSION`, `FUSION` in `data.ts`.
- **Missile defence: the Interceptor Battery** (#777, s2 of #759). From the Industrial Age every people may learn **Interceptors** at the Siege Workshop (500 food, 700 gold, 250 stone, 60 seconds), then raise **Interceptor Batteries** (I in the military menu; 150 wood, 250 stone, 300 gold). A battery covers 12 tiles round it, a teal circle every player sees (the battery itself is seen through the fog, as a silo is), shown while a warhead is in the air, while placing one, or with a silo or battery selected. Two seconds before a rival warhead lands, the nearest charged battery whose cover holds the warhead's ring *as its team is shown it* fires by itself; the roll (60%) is drawn then and kept on the launch, so a save, load or replay never draws it again. One second later the interceptor meets the missile on its arc: a hit is a white burst overhead and no blast and no fallout; a miss and the warhead flies on. One attempt for each warhead however many batteries cover it, the charge spent hit or miss, then a 90 second reload: a salvo's three warheads draw three attempts, and one charge answers one. Tides peoples, who raise no silo, still get the battery. The computer learns Interceptors once a rival reaches the Industrial Age, covers its Town Centers with up to two batteries, and aims its own warheads outside a rival's charged cover when a target as good lies there.
- **Missile defence: the silo held in Reserve** (#781, s3 of #759). Select a Missile Silo and press R to **hold it in Reserve**: it launches nothing (neither a strike nor a salvo) while held, and 30 seconds later it stands ready. Two seconds before a rival warhead lands that no Interceptor Battery answered, anywhere on the map, the nearest ready Reserve silo fires a warhead of its people's store at it, at the same 60% chance; the answer spends that warhead and a launch's fuel, hit or miss, and the silo stands ready again 30 seconds later. Batteries answer first, as they are cheaper, and each warhead still draws one attempt. Standing down is at once. A held silo is public: a teal band round its collar, its doors raised on guard, its beacon amber while it arms and teal once ready, and the HUD says so to every player. A quick Fission clock (25 seconds) can land before a Reserve set at its launch stands ready. The computer holds its last silo in Reserve while a rival stands a silo and it stands two, or its store is full, and stands it down whenever it is the only silo that can strike.
- **Age of Aeronauts.** An Industrial people with a completed Airfield discovers a specific future at its Town Center: 600 food, 500 wood and 800 gold, 60 seconds. Fighters and bombers cost 25% less and take 25% less time to train; foot, ranged, cavalry and siege units cost 20% more. Combat strength stays Industrial, so riflemen, towers, ironclads and rival fighters remain answers. Sky blue bands and rooftop windsocks mark its towns, with its own synthesized theme. Return to Industrial for 200 food and 200 wood in 30 seconds, or Step Back as usual. Hard and Expert computers consider the road once they have aircraft, and leave if their completed airfields are lost. Its aircraft fly on fuel (sorties): rearmed and mended within 4 tiles of one of your Airfields, turned for home 30 tiles out and burning past 36, so a long reach needs a forward Airfield (a Hard or Expert computer raises one toward an enemy its aircraft could not reach and return from, and parks its idle fighters over it). Select one of your aircraft and the fuel bands show round your nearest Airfields: green where it is mended, amber where it turns home, red where it burns.
- **Age of Lightning** (#231). The other future out of the Industrial, the one history almost took (Tesla at Colorado Springs, Wardenclyffe): power is broadcast through the air from coils, not carried by coal and belts. A finished Watch Tower is the clue; the deed is the old way of sending word, your towers having lit 6 beacons (relays count); then 700 food, 450 stone and 1300 gold, 75 seconds (a hard gate). Every Watch Tower becomes a **Coil Tower** with a copper winding and a mushroom crown: buildings within 10 tiles of one train and research 35% faster, buildings outside every field 25% slower, so the town is laid out around its coils and a coil that falls darkens its quarter. A coil's bolt leaps from enemy to enemy (up to 4, 2.5 tiles a leap, 70% kept at each), the hardest on crowds. What it gives up: aircraft, since the air hums with current. A whole-tone theremin plays. Hard and Expert computers with towers and no aircraft light beacons toward it, take it, raise coils by dark workshops, and come back to the Industrial when their coils fall. A faint blue-white ring shows each finished coil's field (and the field a Watch Tower you are placing would add), and a building's panel says whether it is lit ("works 35% faster") or dark. Within 12 tiles of the view a coil hums, louder the nearer (synthesized, `coilHum`). The computer raids the coil whose fall would darken the most of a rival's buildings. `LIGHTNING`, `AI_LIGHTNING` in `data.ts`.

- **The tower line.** A new Watch Tower is light: 250 hit points, 5-damage arrows every 2 seconds, 7 tiles of reach, and it gains only 10% an epoch by itself. Each tower is raised on its own: select it and press **Q** to raise it to a **Guard Tower** (Bronze Age; 100 wood, 50 stone; +2 arrows, +50% hit points), then a **Keep** (Middle Ages; 100 wood, 125 stone; +2 arrows, +1 range, +50%) and a **Bastion** (Gunpowder Age; 100 wood, 75 gold, 175 stone; +2 arrows, +40%). Each step shows on the tower: a timber gallery braced out from the walls, then a stone skirt with battlements and corner buttresses, then a star-fort platform with a cannon in each angled point (`docs/screenshots/tower-line.jpg`, `scripts/gallery/towers.html`). It keeps shooting while it is raised, and its panel shows its name, reach and arrow strength. Fletching and Rifling still add to every tower's arrows, Masonry to their walls. A tower rush is cheap to answer; the tower at the choke point is the one worth the stone. Hard and Expert computers raise their towers, lowest first. `GUNS`, `TOWER_LEVELS` in `data.ts`.
- **Signal beacons.** Select a finished Watch Tower and press **F** to light a fire on its roof (30 wood, 45 seconds, then two minutes before it can be lit again). The flame relays free to your other finished Watch Towers within 14 tiles, and on down a chain, for 25 seconds each: a line of towers carries the warning across your land, and shows rivals every one. The tower sees 7 tiles farther while it burns, but every rival sees the ground around it, so you trade a tower's hiding place for warning. A clever computer lights one when three rival soldiers close in on a tower, or when it sees a rival's beacon burning within 24 tiles of one: it answers your flame.
- **The Rallying Cry.** Select your hero and press **R**: every soldier of yours within 8 tiles is stirred for 12 seconds, striking 30% harder, taking 1 less damage a blow and mended 6 at once. The hero then needs 70 seconds to draw breath. It does nothing for a soldier who isn't fighting, so call it when the lines meet. A clever computer's hero cries out when four of its soldiers stand by it and two rivals are in reach. Stirred soldiers wear a gold ring while it lasts. Numbers in `RALLY` (`src/sim/data.ts`).
- **Citizens run from danger.** Struck by a soldier, one of your citizens dashes into the nearest Town Center or tower within 14 tiles (or runs 8 tiles away if none has room) and comes out once nothing armed and hostile is near; shot at by a tower or Town Center, one steps out of its reach and stands idle. A button on the Town Center turns it off for all your citizens, and one on a selected citizen for just those (a citizen's own setting beats the global one). Only your own citizens: the computer's stand their ground, and the Warband Way, which never shelters, can only run. No save change.
- **A hero's fall.** When a hero dies, yours or one you can see, two gold rings spread over the ground, embers rise and a low bell tolls twice. Look and sound only.
- **Hunters hunted.** On Hard and Expert, the computer falls on your citizens working far from home. When its soldiers see one of yours out in the open (butchering a far kill, digging a ruin, claiming a landmark, mining wild gold), more than 12 tiles from your Town Centers and Watch Towers, with no soldier of yours within 7 tiles and no more than two citizens per raider round them, it sends two or three idle soldiers at them between its waves, chases on to the next one near, and goes home the moment one of your soldiers comes, or after 45 seconds. Seeing no one, it walks a band out to a Hunting Camp of yours it has seen, to find who's butchering there, and if the camp stands empty it sets about the camp itself (45 seconds more) and goes home once it falls. So the far mammoth is a gamble: guard the hunt, hunt nearer home, or take the risk. Not on Easy or Normal, and never during a peace treaty. Numbers in `AI_PROWL` (`src/sim/data.ts`).
- **Signal beacons.** Select a finished Watch Tower and press **F** to light a fire on its roof (30 wood, 45 seconds, then two minutes before it can be lit again). The flame relays free to your other finished Watch Towers within 14 tiles, and on down a chain, for 25 seconds each: a line of towers carries the warning across your land, and shows rivals every one. The tower sees 7 tiles farther while it burns, but every rival sees the ground around it, so you trade a tower's hiding place for warning. A clever computer lights one when three rival soldiers close in on a tower.
- **Borders.** Every finished Town Center, Watch Tower, Wonder, Dock and Airfield claims the land around it (14 tiles for a Town Center); each tile goes to the nearest claimant. Your land and a rival's are drawn as thin coloured lines over the ground you have explored; **O** hides them. **Attrition:** a land soldier in an enemy's land with none of its own team's land within 9 tiles loses 2% of its health every 5 s (not during a peace treaty; ships, aircraft, citizens, priests and traders are exempt), so push out a Town Center or Tower as a foothold. The computer reads this: a wave worn under half health deep in your land, with no foe near, falls back to regroup (`AI_RAID`). **Building:** nothing may be raised in an enemy's land except a Watch Tower (the foothold); neutral and allied land are open. Numbers in `BORDERS` and `ATTRITION`.
- **Tactical Reach.** Hold **Alt** to show pale cyan firing guides for your selected ranged troops, siege, warships, aircraft and completed defenses, alongside all health bars. Dashed amber circles mark siege dead zones. Tower and Town Center placement previews show their future firing reach automatically, including researched range. Guides follow hills and water, stop at currently hidden ground, and show at most 24 selections (the placement preview gets priority). Unit guides measure reach to a target's near edge; target size and type still matter. Presentation only, with no combat, save or replay change.

- **Dust of the march.** Cavalry, chariots and siege engines at a hard pace kick up puffs of tan dust off dry ground, white powder off snow; rain keeps it down and mist halves it. Look only; off on Low quality.
- **Scout Flags.** Ctrl+right-click explored ground or the minimap to leave a small gold pennant and a persistent map triangle; add Shift for a red danger flag. Repeat on a flag to remove it or change its kind. Keep up to six; a seventh replaces the oldest. Mark ruins, ambush sites and safe roads without issuing orders. Flags are private to this browser's current match, never pierce fog or travel online, and reset between matches; saves and replays stay unchanged.
- **Waymarks.** The minimap remembers ruins and relics as you last saw them. A ruin you saw dug shows hollow, struck through and rimmed in the digger's colour; one you saw standing stays standing until a scout looks again; a relic out of sight sits dimmed where it was last seen. Send a scout back to check. Held in the browser, never in saves, replays or online play.
- **Camera bookmarks.** Remember up to four views in a match with **Ctrl+Shift+1–4**, and return to any with **Shift+1–4**. Glance from your town to a frontier fort, trading road or landing beach without changing selected troops, orders or ordinary 1–9 control groups. Views clear when a new match starts; they aren't part of saves or replays.
- **The hours of the day.** A match opens on a morning chosen from its seed, and the light drifts through noon, a golden afternoon, a red sunset and a blue night, one whole day for every hour of play (so a half-hour match runs from morning to dusk). Sun angle, shadows, sky, haze, the water's glint and the sun's colour all follow the hour; the weather greys it afterwards. It is drawn from the match's tick alone, so an online room, a save and a replay all share it; no rules or save change. Add `?hour=19.5` to the address to hold the clock still. **Moonlit nights:** each match keeps a moon phase from its seed, so a full-moon night is brighter than a crescent one, and on a clear night the lakes glint with the stars (cloud hides them). **Lit windows:** windows, torches and lanterns shine near twice as bright after dusk, so a town glows against the dark. **Fireflies:** on a clear night, green-gold sparks drift low over the land near the camera, each blinking at its own pace (off on Low quality, and in rain, snow or mist). **Bats:** at dusk and dawn, a few dark bats flit and jink over the land near the camera, wings beating (clear weather, not on Low quality). **Owls:** in the dead of a clear night, a few pale owls glide in slow circuits over the land near the camera on broad, slow wings, and now and then one hoots from the dark (not on Low quality). **Gulls:** by day, with open water near the view, a gull now and then cries from one side (clear weather). **Birdsong:** by day, away from open water and armies, a songbird whistles now and then from one side (clear weather). **Crickets:** on a clear night, away from open water, crickets chirp in the grass. **Frogs:** on a clear night, with open water near the view, frogs croak at the shore (crickets keep the dry meadow). **Owl perches:** in the dead of night an owl sits on each nearby standing ruin you have seen, blinking its eyes; a dug-out ruin goes quiet. **Carrion crows:** by day, black crows wheel over each carcass in sight that has begun to spoil, a tell that wolves may come, and land to hop about it once the meat is nearly gone (clear weather, not on Low quality), and now and then one caws from over the carcass, louder the nearer (synthesized). **Spoiled meat:** a carcass past its grace lies greyed, ribs showing, its pool gone dark, so you can read what the crows are circling.
- **Weather.** Every match is played under a day chosen from its seed and its land, so everyone in an online room, a save and a replay see the same one: clear, **rain** (streaks, a grey sky, a hiss over the ambience, and in a storm lightning that lights the land and thunder that rolls in a moment later), **snow** (flakes drifting, a muffled hush) or **mist** (the horizon closes in; it breathes, thick at the start, thinning to a third and thickening again every 8 minutes, and the ground the camera looks at is never hazed). Highlands snow, forests rain, plains are mostly fair. The opening banner says what it is. It changes only how the land looks and sounds, never the rules: units see and shoot as far as on a clear day. Each match also has a **wind**, fixed by the seed (stronger in a storm): chimney, forge and battle smoke lean the same way across the land, and rain slants and snow drifts with it. **Banners** on buildings, the airfield's windsock, **masthead pennants on ships** and the pole banners of riders and siege engines stream downwind and ripple faster in a gale (they hang limp on a calm day). Low graphics draw no rain or snow. Add `?weather=rain` (or `snow`, `mist`, `clear`) to the address to try one.
- **Readable health bars.** Quarter marks make remaining health readable by shape as well as color; hold **Alt** to compare all health bars during a battle.

- **Six epochs.** Stone, Bronze, Middle Ages, Gunpowder, Industrial and Atomic. Advancing costs food, wood, gold and stone and takes time at the Town Center. When you advance, every unit re-arms (clubman to spearman to pikeman to halberdier to bayonet-armed fusilier) and every building is rebuilt in the new style, keeping its share of health. The Industrial Age brings riflemen, hussars, field guns, a Field Marshal, red-brick towns with smokestacks, ironclads, paddle steamers and aircraft.
- **Deep veins.** Some worlds hold **rare ores** that others lack, by the seed's own dice: **salt**, **saltpetre** and **blood-iron**. Each lies in a few neutral veins out between the towns, clear of every home, where every town can walk. Citizens mine them like stone and bring them home to a separate store shown on the top bar; click a vein for what it is. Salt and blood-iron open roads (the Age of Merchants, Iron and Blood); saltpetre lets the Siege Workshop research **Black Powder** (ranged +1, siege +15%) once 40 are mined, and blood-iron lets the Forge research **Blood-Forged Blades** (melee +1, armor +1) once 30 are mined, each only in a world that holds the ore (#356). Salt has a use at home too: **Salt Curing** (Storehouse, 30 mined) makes hunting 25% and foraging 15% faster and lets citizens carry 2 more. **Selling ore:** a Market buys a lot of 20 of any rare ore you hold for a flat price (salt 40, saltpetre 55, blood-iron 90 gold; the ore can't be bought back), and the ore sold still counts toward the doors it opens. A clever computer with a Market sells what it digs at once. `ORE_TRADE` in `data.ts`.
- **Caves.** Where a world holds a rare ore, one or two caves (three on a large map) are cut into the ground between the towns: a single tile of the ore, three times a vein's worth, shown as a dark cave mouth. On medium and large maps about half are **dens** where a wolf pack lairs at the mouth, so the richest ore is the best guarded (#356).
- **Ore gates.** In a world that holds the ore, mining it is the only door: **60 salt** opens the Age of Merchants, **50 blood-iron** opens Iron and Blood, and trade or plunder alone no longer does. The road says nothing of the ore until you have dug some. A world without the ore keeps the usual deeds (400 gold of trade, 600 plunder), so a simple road always works, and both are side roads, so a town with no vein in reach just stays on the trunk. The computer digs for them (#356).
- **The computer digs the ore.** A clever town bound for the Merchants or Iron and Blood, in a world that holds the ore, sends `AI_ORE.crew` citizens to the nearest vein once it has a Storehouse, holds the road while they dig, and sends them back to work when the door opens or the road is given up. Without the ore it walks the deeds as before.
- **Swords for hire** (the Age of Merchants, #37). A soldier hired at the Market is paid by the day, not once: after the hire price has covered its first 45 seconds, each draws 2 gold every 15 seconds from the treasury, and the unit panel marks it 💰 Hired. A purse too thin to pay leaves it a payday behind (a toast warns you, the mark turns *Unpaid*); one left unpaid twice walks off the field for good. Wages go on after you leave the Age of Merchants. A quick army now, against a treasury you must keep fed, so raiding a merchant's traders or Market starves his swords. The computer hires only with six paydays per sword left in its purse. Browser unverified.
- **Ages as a web.** The five epochs are the main road, and it always works. Beside it, side ages branch off and rejoin it. The first is the **Age of Merchants**: once a Bronze Age people has a Market and its traders have brought home 400 gold, the Town Center (**Y**) can take it there instead of the Middle Ages. Same soldiers and technologies, but the Market **hires mercenaries** (foot, bow and horse) for gold alone in half the time, trade pays a quarter more, and soldiers trained the usual way cost a fifth more. A merchant people builds no Stable: cavalry is for hire at the Market or not at all (it may stable horses again in the Middle Ages or Gunpowder). Bog iron dug on any road (75 bars a step, to +40%) lifts trade pay in the trading ages (Merchants, Fairs, Sail), so a people that dug iron early walks into them richer. From there, on to Gunpowder (more gold, less stone) or back onto the Middle Ages. Your age's name shows on the top bar and the scoreboard, so rivals can read the road you took. Each side age **looks and sounds like itself**: awnings on merchants' Markets and houses, rust-brown and smithy glow in the Iron Age, banners, lamps and pale stone for pilgrims, striped stalls and bunting in the Age of Fairs, each with a theme of its own.
- **Crafts of the side ages.** Like the Bloomery, two more technologies work only on their own road and lapse when the people leaves it: the **Ropewalk** (Dock, Age of Sail: ships 15% sturdier, warships +1 range) and **Letters of Credit** (Market, Age of Merchants: technologies cost 15% less).
- **Heirlooms.** A people remembers the side ages it has stood in, and two roads walked teach together what neither teaches alone: a craft that is **kept for good** once learned, on whatever road comes next. The **Bell Foundry** (Temple; Iron Age + Age of Pilgrims): relics pay 50% more, priests 20% faster. **Iron-Bound Hulls** (Dock; Iron + Sail): ships 30% sturdier, fishing 20% faster. The **Joint-Stock Company** (Market; Merchants + Sail): trade trips pay 15% more, traders 10% faster. The **Masons' Lodge** (Temple; Pilgrims + Reclaimers): buildings go up 20% faster and are 15% sturdier. **St. Elmo's Fire** (Temple; Pilgrims + Lightning, #240): a Temple within a Watch Tower's reach mends the soldiers near it, so an army is staged between a coil and a shrine, on any road. **Wireless Telegraphy** (Dock; Sail + Lightning, #240): ships and Docks within a Watch Tower's reach see 5 tiles farther, so a coil on the headland is a lookout over the water. An heirloom shows greyed at its building once you've walked one of its ages, naming the other, and a toast and a slow bell peal tell you when it opens. Once learned it shows: a bronze bell in a timber cote on the Temple's ridge, iron bands and bolts on the Dock, a gilt-sealed counting-house board and coin stacks at the Market, shear-legs hoisting a dressed block and stacked ashlar at the Temple. The computer plans for them: once it has walked one side age, it takes the second road that opens an heirloom with it, and buys the craft first, even at a busy Dock. It reads yours too: against a Joint-Stock Company its riders lie in wait by your trading post on any road, and against a Masons' Lodge it keeps one more siege engine. **Galvanic Revival** (Workshop; Reclaimers + Lightning, #240): repairs inside a Watch Tower's reach cost nothing and go half as fast again, and each ancient ruin you dug out in a field pays 25 gold every 30 s, so a forward coil beside a ruin becomes a mine. The Path of Blood has two of its own (#355 s6). **Galvanic Blood** (Workshop; Lightning + Hemocraft): a grafted soldier in a Watch Tower's reach is fed as at a Transfusion Station, in any age, so a grafted army outlives Hemocraft wherever the towers stand, and a rival who fells the towers starves it; a glass tank of blood wound with copper wire stands by the Workshop. **Military Orders** (Temple; Pilgrims + Warband): a soldier within 6 tiles of one of your priests is sworn (⚜ in its panel): no rival's priest can turn, offer up or curse it, and your priests mend soldiers half again as fast, so the priests march with the army; a red war banner, a kite shield and a sword stand by the Temple. The computer reads it: facing a sworn army, two of its riders go for the priest who swears it (#459).
- **Litany.** A third craft of the pilgrims' road (Temple, 100 food 120 gold, Age of Pilgrims only): priests chant over a rival's building 30% faster; the pilgrim computer buys it. Lost when the people leaves the road.

- **Take it back.** Salt, saltpetre and blood-iron you have mined are kept in your Town Center and Storehouses, and are safe only while those stand. When one falls, half of what you hold spills out (shared across the stores still standing) as a split sack on the ground, and a fallen digger drops its basket. Anyone's citizens can carry it home: select them and right-click the ore, 20 a trip. What you carry home counts toward your own ore doors (Black Powder, Salt Curing, the Merchant shortcut), and what you lose sets yours back. Spread your ore across Storehouses, or sell it before a raid. Assayed, you see every spill on the minimap. The computer carries home ore lying near its town, and once it has assayed it marches on the store of the rival holding the most.
- **Posts that sell what the map lacks.** On a world without salt or blood-iron, a trading post sells it (its heart on the minimap wears the ore's colour, and your Prospector's Lodge gives the price). Select traders and press **Buy** (Z): they pay gold at the post for each load of 10 (salt 30 gold, blood-iron 68, dearer by 10% with every load anyone buys, so a rival can bid it up on you), carry it home to the Market, and go again until stopped. Bought ore counts as mined: 60 salt opens the road to the Age of Merchants, 50 blood-iron the road to Iron and Blood, without their deeds. A trader felled on the way drops its load for anyone to carry off. The computer buys for a road it has chosen whose ore its world lacks, and its riders fall on rival traders carrying key ore.

- **Bog iron and the Iron Age.** Some worlds hold **bog iron**, rust-red ore in marsh mud, and some don't: the seed decides. Where it lies, every town has a patch the same distance out. A miner whose seam runs dry takes a free Mine Shaft nearby before standing idle; if three or more still stand idle for 5 seconds, a notice offers **Find more stone** (or gold) to send them to the nearest other seam or shaft. **Building** is a destination too: the hands join the nearest unfinished building (five to a site before the next gets any) and go back to their old work after. And when a button is too dear, clicking it says what is missing with a **Send N** button per resource, N being the hands that cover the shortfall in about 30 seconds. `reassign` in `src/sim/labor.ts`, `LABOR` in `data.ts`; no save change.
- **Hands find their own work** (#344). Your citizens look for the next job before they stand idle. A new citizen with no rally point goes to the nearest bushes, game or trees. A rally flag set on a tree, bush, carcass or shoal keeps meaning "that resource here" after the tile is used up, so later citizens take the nearest of its kind by the flag (two to a node) instead of standing on bare ground. A hunter done with a carcass spears the next deer within 8 tiles. A picker whose bushes and game are gone takes an unworked field within 12 tiles, and two pickers never race for one field. A field takes one farmer: the Workers panel sends no second hand to a field still going up, and builders called off other work to raise a field together leave one of them farming it and go back to what they were doing. The computer sets its own people to work, so it is unchanged. `AUTO_WORK` in `data.ts`; `npx tsx scripts/idle-audit.ts` counts where a plain player's citizens still fall idle, and `npx tsx scripts/town-audit.ts [civ|all]` does the same for a fuller town of any people (storehouses, mines, a barracks, the Workers panel).
- **Patrol and explore** (#454). Select soldiers, scouts, priests, ships or aircraft. **Q** then a click on the map sends them back and forth between where they stand and that spot, each keeping its place in the group; **Shift-click** adds more waypoints to the round (up to 8). **E** sends them to explore: each heads for the nearest ground your people have never seen, then the next, fanning out from one another, and stops when nothing it can reach is left unseen. On either, soldiers fight by their stance: those told to attack and destroy fall on whatever they see, those who fight if provoked only on what comes close, those holding only on what is in reach, and runners step away from armed foes and take up the round again. Then they take up the round again. Click a **trading post** (or one of your traders) after **Q** to guard that road: they walk the trader's way between the nearest Market and the post. Explorers keep clear of enemy towers they have seen, and report a rival army in sight ("Scouts report an army of N"; Space to look). `PATROL` and `EXPLORE` in `data.ts`. The computer does it too: from 2½ minutes a clever opponent sends one rider exploring (so it learns where you are) and posts two soldiers to patrol its trade road, or the border toward you (`AI_SCOUT`).
- **Orders spread** (#266). Right-click a tree, bush, mine or shoal with a crowd and they fan over nearby nodes of that kind (two to a node, within 5 tiles of the one you clicked) instead of piling onto one; attack a visible foe with a crowd and three go for it while the rest take the nearest foes within 4 tiles. Carcasses, farms and buildings still take everyone, and the computer's orders are unchanged.
- **The wild.** The Stone Age map pushes back. A **mammoth** grazes out from every town (and a few more in the open): a feast, its carcass holds 600 food, but it doesn't bolt when speared. It turns on whoever threw and charges, goring for a few seconds after every wound. A lone hunter dies; five citizens, or a couple of soldiers in front, bring it down. **Wolf packs** keep dens out between the towns, often by wild gold: they roam near the den and fall on anyone who comes near, then go home. A lone citizen won't survive them; a few soldiers or a band of citizens clear a den (they leave nothing to eat). Bitten or gored people fight back, then go back to work. Soldiers on foot or horse can be sent after any beast. The computer sends a hunting party after its mammoth and soldiers to clear the dens near its town. A **wild boar** stands guard by a berry patch 7–15 tiles from each town (more in the open on bigger maps): it charges anyone within a few tiles and gives up past its leash. One citizen loses to it; three win, and the carcass (260 food) pays better than a deer, so the second berry patch is a choice: spear the boar, or gather elsewhere. The computer sends three citizens once it has six. **The wild teems** (`TEEM`): beyond those, every town has a second mammoth 28 tiles out and a wolf pack of its own about 37 tiles out on its side of the map (past where the computer clears wolves by habit, so you meet that pack when you choose to), and the open ground between the towns holds more mammoths, boars (by berries or at a wood's edge) and wolf dens; about twice the big game there was. On the minimap a mammoth is a big brown mark, a boar a rust one and wolves are red. **Herds drift** and the wild thins as you grow: every 2½ minutes a deer herd moves 7–12 tiles to fresh ground, never onto land inside anyone's border, so the easy hunting by your first town runs out as your borders widen and a hunter must follow the herd (the computer does). **Wolves hunt deer** that graze within 7 tiles of their den and leave the carcass where it fell: spear it from under the pack's noses, or let the wolves thin the herd. **Meat spoils**: a carcass keeps 40 seconds, then rots away over the next 2½ minutes (a mammoth loses about 4 food a second), so a hunt is a race to the butchering: send enough hands, and don't fell game far from home. **Drying Racks** (Town Center, Stone Age, 60 food 60 wood) cut the rot to a quarter on land inside your borders; the computer buys them once big game is down. **Carrion:** once a carcass has begun to turn, a wolf pack within 15 tiles of its den scents it, comes and eats it (about 3 food a second a wolf), and bites whoever is butchering it, so a kill on land nobody holds is a race: butcher fast, bring spears, or clear the den first. Meat inside a border is safe. A **Hunting Camp** (economy menu, **N**; 45 wood, Stone Age) takes in food and claims a small patch of land (4 tiles) round it: raise one by a far kill or where the herd has gone, and the butchers carry the meat a short way, wolves leave it alone and Drying Racks keep it; it is frail, and the computer builds one by a big kill far from its food drops. **Hounds** (Hunting Camp, 80 food 40 wood): tamed dogs run beside your hunters, so game is butchered 25% faster and wolves, boars and mammoths do half their damage to your people; the computer buys them once big game is down. **Seasons:** the year turns every 2½ minutes, from spring (every match opens in spring, so the first winter comes at 7½ minutes); the icon by the clock shows the season. **Summer** berries gather 25% faster but meat rots 1.5× as fast; **winter** berries gather at 55% and fields at 75%, meat keeps twice as long, and wolves and boars notice your people from 40% further. So forage in the warm, stock food and Drying Racks before winter, and hunt in the cold with a few spears. The clever computer plans for winter: from autumn it raises extra fields, puts 30% more of its people on food, goes after mammoths and boars with a smaller town, and in winter clears wolves with one fewer soldier. **The land shows the season:** spring meadows are fresh green, summer grass goes gold, broadleaf trees turn orange, red and gold in autumn (each tree its own colour, a few late), and in winter the grass frosts, broadleaves go bare and snow settles on the ground, tree tops and roofs over the first seconds of winter, melting in spring; evergreens stay green and team colours stay clear of snow. Each season fades into the next over its last 20 seconds, and **the minimap wears it too**: meadow and woods go gold, ochre and frosted, and snow whitens the map's grass as it settles. **Lakes freeze:** as the snow settles, ice grows out from the shore over the shallows (the deep middle stays open water), on the land and on the minimap, and melts back in spring. **Winter sounds:** the crickets, frogs and songbirds fall silent, and a thin, hollow wind swells and sinks through the quiet (the gulls stay). Add `?season=winter` (or `spring`, `summer`, `autumn`) to the address to hold one. (The wild is not in campaign chapters; the seasons are.)
- **An economy.** Citizens gather berries, wood, stone and gold, hunt deer (they bolt when speared) and butcher the carcasses, and carry it all to a Town Center or a Storehouse. Farms never run out. Houses make room for 5 more people (up to 150). A **Market** trades food, wood and stone for gold, with prices that move as people buy and sell and drift back toward their start over time (a point every 10 seconds), so dumping a glut hurts for a while and then fades. The Market panel shows recovery arrows; buy/sell tooltips show the baseline quote and explain shared prices.
- **Seventeen buildings:** Town Center, House, Farm, Storehouse, Market, Temple, Wonder, Wall, Gate, Barracks, Archery Range, Stable, Forge, Siege Workshop, Watch Tower, Dock and Airfield. Drag to lay out a wall (log palisades in the Stone Age, stone after); set gates into your walls, which open for you and your allies only.
- **Thirty technologies** at the Town Center, Storehouse, Forge, Barracks, Stable, Temple, Workshop, Dock, Airfield and Market: better tools, wheelbarrows and handcarts, the plow and irrigation, masonry, weapon and armor lines, conscription, theology, chemistry, gill nets, the lighthouse, shipwrights, rifling, the steam engine, aviation, caravans, banking and pilgrimage (relics in a Temple pay double).
- **The counter triangle and more.** Infantry beat cavalry, archers beat infantry, cavalry beat archers, priests and siege. **Siege engines** (battering rams, catapults that crush groups, bombards) break walls and towns. **Priests** heal your wounded and convert enemy soldiers. One **hero** at a time inspires the soldiers around them to hit harder and heal.
- **Relics.** A few gilded relics lie out on every map: one at the same distance from each town, more out between the towns. From the Bronze Age a **priest** can carry one home to a **Temple**, where it brings in 30 gold a minute for as long as the Temple stands. Hands full, the priest can't heal or convert (**D** sets it down); if they fall, the relic drops where they stood, and a fallen Temple spills its relics. Keep **every relic** on the map in your side's temples as long as a wonder must stand, and you win.
- **Trade.** Two or three neutral **trading posts** stand out near the edge of every map, as far from one town as from the next. From the Bronze Age a **Market** trains **traders** (a pack donkey, then an ox cart, a covered wagon and a steam traction engine). Right-click a post and they run the road on their own: goods out, a chest of gold home. A trip pays by the square of the distance from the Market to the post (a Market far from the post pays far more), but nothing is paid until the trader is home, and cavalry ride traders down. **A post's market floods** (#493): every load, yours or a rival's, cuts that post's price by 12% (down to half), and it heals about 1 point in 33 seconds, so one trader in 40 seconds keeps a post at full price and a crowd on one road does not. Spread the traders over the posts, or work the one your rival has flooded; the post's panel shows the glut, and the computer moves a trader to a fresher post when it pays a fifth more. **Caravans** and **Banking** at the Market speed them up and raise the pay. A Market's rally point on a post sends new traders straight onto the road. **Allies trade together:** every ally with a Market of their own takes a quarter of each trip a teammate's trader brings home, so a team that builds Markets side by side out-earns one that doesn't. A trader whose owner has no Market standing can set out from an ally's instead (never a rival's). **By sea:** a trading post with water within seven tiles is a **port**, and from the Middle Ages a Dock trains **merchant ships** (galley, cog, East Indiaman, steam freighter). Right-click a port and they sail the water road from the Dock, a fifth richer than the land road and out of cavalry's reach, but open to warships. On the Inland Sea map every post stands on the shore, so every post is a port. Hard and Expert computers keep a merchant ship or two sailing when they have a Dock and a port. The **Lighthouse** (a Dock technology after Gillnets) lets merchant ships reach posts four tiles farther from the water.
- **The tide of battle.** Over every fight your people are in floats a thin bar, your colour against the foe's, with a word for how it stands (*Crushing*, *Winning*, *Even*, *Losing*, *Routed*) and an arrow for which way it swings. It weighs hit points against damage a second (Lanchester's square law), counters, armour, veterancy, heroes and towers included, but only the foes you can see: an army waiting in the fog is still a surprise. Click a bar to pick up your fighters there, to pull them back before a rout or pour them in. When a fight you aren't looking at turns from even to losing, a sagging horn sounds, the minimap flashes and Space looks. `BATTLE` in `data.ts`, `src/sim/battles.ts`; no save change.
- **Veterancy.** A soldier who defeats enemy soldiers earns ranks: Veteran (3 kills), Elite (8) and Champion (15), shown as ▲ in the selection panel. Each rank makes their blows hit harder (10%, 20%, 30%) and, from Elite, adds armor. Veterans wear gold chevrons over their heads (one per rank); a rank-up rings a chime, sparkles and pings the minimap. Keep your survivors alive: a veteran line is worth more than fresh recruits.
- **Shelter.** Ring the **town bell** and every citizen runs into the nearest Town Center or tower, which shoot more arrows for each one inside; the all-clear sends them back to the work they left.
- **Control.** Queue orders with Shift, four stances (**Z** attack and destroy, **X** fight if provoked, **C** hold, **N** run: step away from armed foes, never fight back unless ordered), attack-move, formations, control groups, rally points (on a resource, new citizens start gathering), idle-citizen and whole-army hotkeys. **Shift + .** or **Shift-click Idle** selects all available idle citizens; the counter and finder skip citizens in shelter or aboard ships and those with queued work.
- **A computer opponent** at four difficulties: **Easy** for a first game (it gathers slowly, keeps a small town, plays no tricks and sends its first small wave after about 18 minutes, time to reach the Bronze Age and raise a guard), **Normal**, where a new game starts (first wave at 12 minutes, no tricks), **Hard** (8 minutes, every trick) and **Expert** (5½ minutes, a faster economy; `npx tsx scripts/tiers.ts` compares them). It plays through the same commands you do: it balances its economy, hunts, farms, fishes, researches, trades at the market, builds walls of soldiers, rings its bell when raided, brings priests, heroes and siege, counters what you field and attacks in growing waves. On the water it builds warships; in the Industrial Age it flies fighters over its town and sends bombers against yours. It races for a wonder when it can. It saves for a Market when there are trading posts, keeps traders on the road that pays best for the time it takes without passing a rival town (judged along the path they actually walk, which bends around water and hills), keeps off a road for a few minutes after losing a trader on it, trains none when no road is safe, and its riders cut rival trade roads near home. Hard and Expert computers **walk the web of ages**: a people that fights on foot has the land surveyed and digs its way into the Iron Age (never the Mongols, who won't give up the horse); on a map with trading posts it may raise its Market early in the Bronze Age and take the merchants' road, then hire soldiers at the Market; it turns pilgrim when its Temples keep relics (and turns back onto the Middle Ages if they're taken), and takes the Fairs when it trades too. It prepares for a road without starving its war: it surveys once its town has grown, digs harder while it waits on the deed and sends the diggers back to work once it's dug, raises an early Market only when its army isn't owed soldiers, and an Iron Age army without horses answers massed archers with its own bows. A beaten computer doesn't freeze in its age: once a wave has gone out, it waits a minute and a half at most to raise the next before it climbs anyway; its citizens come back out to work when a straggler or two is all that's left at its gates; and it holds a little longer for a road whose deed is nearly done. It reads your road: against an Iron people it masses archers, and against merchants a band of riders lies in wait by the trading post nearest your town.
- **Match options:** your people and colour, up to three rivals, a computer ally, six map types (Continental, Highlands, Lakes, Forest, Plains and the Inland Sea, a ring of land round a sea full of fish), three sizes, the starting epoch, a peace treaty, and three ways to play: **Conquest**, **Regicide** (each people has a King; lose them and you lose) and **Deathmatch** (everyone starts rich). Win by destroying every rival town: a people whose **last Town Center** falls (a finished one, a foundation, or one packed on a steppe wagon all count) has 45 seconds to raise another, or it is lost. The Town Center has 2,800 health, a struck one raises its own alert ("Your Town Center is under attack!"), and a second one built far from the first is the answer: the computer builds one too, 24 tiles from home on the side away from its rival, once it has left the Stone Age (Easy waits for the Middle Ages) and can spare the wood and stone (`HEART`, `AI_HEART` in `data.ts`; not in Regicide or campaign chapters), by keeping a **Wonder** standing until its countdown ends, or by keeping every relic as long. A Wonder can't be raised in secret: the moment its foundation is laid every player hears a far-off horn and sees a banner (Space jumps to the site), and the top bar shows how far along it is, so the whole build can be fought over. A Hard or Expert computer guards its own Wonder while it rises or counts down: part of its army stays by it, out of the attack waves, and any soldier of yours within 26 tiles of it draws the whole army (Easy and Normal don't, so a beginner can still break one).
- **Online play.** Make a room, share its code or link, and play with up to three friends and computer players, in teams or every one for themselves, with chat. If someone's connection drops they can reload and rejoin; if they leave, the computer takes over their people, and gives it back when they return. **Pick it up later:** the room keeps a started match for 36 hours after the last player leaves, so everyone can close the game and come back with the same code (pause first and nobody is handed to the computer; an empty room never plays on). A browser that holds no seat, such as another device, picks which away player it comes back as. **Rematch** (#746): the end screen of a room has a Rematch button; when every person connected has pressed it (click again to take it back), the room starts the same match over, same seed, seats and peoples, with the record wiped. A player who has left isn't waited for; watchers follow into the new match. No save change.
- **Watch a live match.** On the online card, type a room's code and press **Watch** (or open `?room=CODE&watch=1`) once its match has begun: you get the whole game from its first turn, can look through everything or any player's eyes from the bar above, and cannot touch it. The room reads nothing from a watcher (no orders, chat, pause or checksums) and holds up to eight.
- **One live connection per player.** Reopening an online room replaces the old tab's connection; the old tab stops retrying, and its queued messages cannot act on your seat. Closing or reconnecting cancels obsolete retries; lobby validation errors still allow recovery from a later network drop.
- **Saves and replays.** Matches save in the browser (and when you leave the page) and can be continued. **Cloud saves:** three slots on the server (Menu → Cloud saves; open them from the title screen), so a match survives a cleared browser and carries to another device: the game makes you a secret 20-letter save key, and entering it on another device opens the same slots. Your Long Road stars follow the key too. **Show QR code** turns the key into a link a phone camera opens (the key rides in the link's `#` part, never sent to a server). No account; a key nobody uses for a year is emptied. Every match is recorded: watch it back at up to 8× speed through anyone's eyes, or download the replay to share.
- **After the match,** a timeline charts everyone's score, population, army and gathering, with the moment each reached a new epoch. Hover a player's epoch in the table for the minute they reached each age.
- **Tips for a first game** point the way, one at a time, and can be turned off.
- **Fog of war** shared by allies; enemy buildings stay as you last saw them until you look again.
- **Share a match.** The end screen's **Share** button makes a picture of the result and the road you walked through the ages, with a link that opens the same world for a friend (the share sheet on a phone; elsewhere the picture downloads and the text and link are copied). Room invites and world links show their own preview in chat apps.
- **The site around it.** A guide built from the game's own data (`/guide/`, `/peoples/`, `/ages/`, from `src/site/pages.ts`), a sitemap, link-preview cards and app icons; it installs as an app and, once visited, plays offline (`public/sw.js`; online rooms still need the network). A phone or tablet with no mouse is told, once, that the game is made for a mouse and offered the link to send to a computer.

- **Lakes and seas** with turquoise shallows, foam along the shore, waves rolling in and sun glints. Fish shoals glitter at the surface, a fish leaping now and then.

## Run it

Building the game, as a person or an agent? Start with [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md): the rules, the checks and how several agents share the work.

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # http://localhost:5173 (single player)
npm run check      # typecheck + the quick tests: what to run while building (about 20 s)
npm test           # rules, AI, saving and determinism tests (Vitest)
npm run test:slow  # the slow tests in tests/slow/, which CI runs daily
npm run build      # typecheck (game and worker) + production build into dist/
npx tsx scripts/sim.ts 3 medium 30   # a headless computer-vs-computer match, minute by minute
npx tsx scripts/seed-atlas.ts --size small --bog --ruin lore,map   # find seeds with bog iron, ruins and the like (#80)
npx tsx scripts/seed-atlas.ts --size medium --ore salt,bloodiron --no-ore saltpetre --caves 1 --share # rare ores and caves, as in ordinary matches
npx tsx scripts/seed-atlas.ts --size medium --dens 1 --limit 3 # find guarded caves
npx tsx scripts/seed-atlas.ts --survey --size small --count 100   # how often each feature occurs on each map type
npx tsx scripts/seed-atlas.ts --size small --apart 70 --close 90   # only worlds whose nearest two starts lie 70–90 tiles apart
npx tsx scripts/seed-atlas.ts --size small --bog --share   # also print ?seed=… addresses that open the title screen on that world
# On the title screen, the 🔗 beside the map seed copies the same kind of address for the world you have set up.
```

Online play needs the Worker. To try it on your machine: `npm run build && npx wrangler dev`, then open http://localhost:8787 in two browser windows (one of them private, so each has its own player id). With `npm run dev` running as well, the dev server forwards `/api` to `wrangler dev`.

## Put it online on your subdomain (Cloudflare)

The game is a Cloudflare Worker that serves the built files, the same setup as Tideborn, plus a Durable Object for online rooms (one per room; it works on the free plan).

1. In the Cloudflare dashboard, go to **Workers & Pages → Create → Import a repository** and pick `Ages-of-earth`.
2. Set the **build command** to `npm run build` and the **deploy command** to `npx wrangler deploy`.
3. Add a **build variable** `NODE_VERSION` = `22` (Vite 8 needs Node 20.19+ or 22.12+).
4. Once deployed, open the Worker's **Settings → Domains & Routes → Add → Custom domain** and enter the subdomain, for example `ages.yourdomain.com`. Your domain must use Cloudflare for DNS; Cloudflare creates the DNS record and the certificate for you.

Instead of step 4, you can put the subdomain in `wrangler.toml` (there is a commented-out `routes` entry) so it is set on every deploy. The Durable Object binding and its migration are already in `wrangler.toml`; the first deploy creates them.

## How it is built

**Why Three.js.** Before building, I compared the options:

- **Three.js** is small (about 150 KB) and gives full control. It reuses Tideborn's code and deploy setup.
- **Babylon.js** has more built in, but its physics and navigation-mesh crowds aren't deterministic, so lockstep multiplayer would still need custom systems.
- **PlayCanvas's** main draw is its commercial team editor.
- **Godot 4** only has its WebGL 2 "Compatibility" renderer on the web (no Forward+ renderer, no WebGPU yet, no C#).
- **Unity's** empty web builds are about 8–11 MB before any game.

In an RTS the hard parts (a deterministic simulation, pathfinding and lockstep networking) are custom in any engine, and the look comes from lighting, art direction and instancing, which Three.js does well. Custom shader code is kept small and in one place (`render/materials.ts`, `render/water.ts`) so a move to Three.js's WebGPU renderer stays possible later.

**A deterministic simulation.** The rules run in fixed ticks, 20 a second, and the view draws between ticks at the screen's frame rate. Everything a player does becomes a command, and the computer opponent issues the same commands. The rules use only +, −, ×, ÷ and square roots (no `Math.sin`, `atan2`, `pow`, clocks or `Math.random`; a test enforces this), seeded randomness kept in the state, and id-ordered iteration. So the same seed and commands give the same match in every browser. That one property gives three features: saved games (a test checks a saved and reloaded match plays on identically), replays (a setup and a list of commands) and online play.

**Online, lockstep.** Each room is a Durable Object that keeps the clock: every 100 ms it sends every browser the commands that arrived as one numbered turn (two ticks). Each browser plays a turn's ticks only once it has the turn, so all of them run the same match; the room never runs the rules. It stamps each command with its sender's seat (nobody can command someone else's people), keeps every turn so a player who reloads replays back in, compares checksums the browsers report to catch any drift, and hands a departed player's people to the computer after 30 seconds.

**Movement.** Short trips use A* on the tile grid (eight directions, no corner cutting), pulled taut into straight lines. Long trips walk down a distance field grown once from the destination and shared by every unit going there, built with a bucket queue (Dial's algorithm) and cached. Gates let their own side through. Units push apart, slide along walls, re-route when stuck, and groups move in formation. The route work has a budget per tick.

**Rendering.** Models are built from primitives and baked into one geometry each, with colours in the vertices. Units are instanced per model with three per-instance values for animation (leg swing, arm swing, bob). A small shader addition swings tagged limbs about their joints, tints team-coloured parts and applies the fog of war, which is a texture every material reads.

## Code layout

- `src/sim/`: the rules, pure TypeScript with no rendering, so they run headless in tests.
  - `data.ts` every number (epochs, units, buildings, technologies, peoples, counters, costs, gathering rates, AI levels)
  - `types.ts` the state, orders, events and commands
  - `stats.ts` unit and building stats with technologies and the people's bonus applied
  - `mapgen.ts` the six map types: fair starts for 2 to 4 players, lakes and seas, ridges, woods, mines, deer, fish shoals, guaranteed connections
  - `path.ts` A* and shared distance fields; `grid.ts` tile helpers; `spatial.ts` the neighbour grid
  - `units.ts` what units do each tick (orders, gathering, hunting, fishing, building, fighting, healing, converting, sheltering, boarding and landing, walking, sailing and flying)
  - `combat.ts` blows, shots, splash, towers and the damage table; `animals.ts` deer
  - `commands.ts` every player action, checked and applied
  - `vision.ts` fog of war; `ai.ts` the computer opponent; `save.ts` saving to text and back; `relics.ts` relics carried, kept and let go; `trade.ts` trading posts, traders' roads and what a trip pays
  - `game.ts` creating a match and advancing it one tick, victory, score, the checksum
- `src/render/`: the Three.js view.
  - `view.ts` puts it together: lights, shadows, effects for game events, positional sound cues, picking
  - `unitModels.ts`, `buildingModels.ts` and `natureModels.ts` are the models; `kit.ts` the primitive kit and baking
  - `unitsView.ts` instanced, animated units, deer and corpses; `relicsView.ts` relics on the ground, carried and kept; `postsView.ts` the trading posts; `buildingsView.ts` buildings as the player knows them (walls join their neighbours); `resourcesView.ts` woods, mines and carcasses
  - `terrain.ts` the ground, decoration and fog texture; `water.ts` the lakes; `materials.ts` the shared shader additions
  - `overlays.ts` selection rings, health bars, shots, placement and wall previews, rally flag; `camera.ts`, `effects.ts`, `sky.ts`, `post.ts`
- `src/ui/`: `hud.ts` (resource bar, selection panel, command card, timers), `lobby.ts` (online rooms), `tips.ts`, `minimap.ts`, `thumbs.ts`, `icons.ts` and `style.css`.
- `src/audio/`: `sound.ts` the synthesized effects and voices, `music.ts` the generative music.
- `src/net/`: `protocol.ts` what browsers and rooms say to each other, `client.ts` the browser's connection.
- `scripts/campaign.ts` plays every chapter with a computer in your seat that plays toward the chapter's goals (walls, towers, siege, fleets, bombers, holding out, burning the rival Town Center) and reports which goals it reached. A computer wins all six, at Hard or, for the hardest, at Expert (`npx tsx scripts/campaign.ts [normal|hard] [chapter id …]`: the level ids `normal` and `hard` are shown as Hard and Expert since #258). The slow tests `tests/slow/campaign-proof*.test.ts` check all six stay winnable: a chapter passes when a computer wins it on its own map or, at Expert, any of the next fifteen, so one chaotic match can't flip it (#43). `npx tsx scripts/campaign.ts --seeds 8` prints each chapter's win rate over eight maps at Hard and Expert, the way to judge an AI or balance change.
- `src/main.ts`: the game loop, input, menus, saves, replays, online play and the title screen's live match.
- `worker/`: the Cloudflare Worker (`index.ts`) and the room Durable Object (`room.ts`); `wrangler.toml` their settings.
- `tests/`: 83 tests (the campaign's goals, raids, time limits and saving among them, and its chapters beside relics and trade): map fairness and connectivity for every size, type and player count; gathering, hunting, building, farming, training and housing; command checks and refunds; epochs; the counter triangle in every epoch; technologies and peoples; the town bell and sheltering; priests, heroes and siege; the market; wonders; walls and gates; queued orders and stances; peace treaties; game modes; determinism, saving and the no-unsafe-math rule; full computer-vs-computer matches; the computer coming for a player who does nothing; relics placed fairly on every map, carried, kept, dropped, spilled and won with; trading posts placed fairly on every map, traders paid by the road, lost on the way home, finding another Market, rallied onto the road, ridden down by cavalry, saved and reloaded, and the computer trading; docks on the shore, fishing, sea fights and who can hit a ship, boarding and landing across the sea (wherever the ship lies), passengers lost with their ship, the Industrial Age, bombers, fighters and anti-aircraft fire, and determinism and saving with ships and planes.
